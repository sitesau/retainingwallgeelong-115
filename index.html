The strongest first move is a short site brief that any contractor can read in minutes. Those facts do not replace inspection, yet they turn a vague enquiry into a quote that can be compared line by line.   • Existing wall: record lean, cracked units, failed joints, damp patches and where water leaves the site.
  • New wall: describe the slope, retained area, boundary line and structures close to the alignment.
  • Access: state whether a mini excavator, truck and material delivery can reach the work zone.

Match the system to load, water and footprint

Material preference should arrive after the site has spoken. Concrete sleeper walls pair reinforced sleepers with galvanised posts and suit replacement walls, boundaries and stepped house pads. Timber can work for low terraces and garden edges when design life and future renewal are accepted. Masonry block suits reinforced, core filled retention where a compact footprint and rendered finish fit. Segmental blocks use interlock and may form landscaped terraces or engineered reinforced soil walls where foundations, drainage and geogrid are designed together. Rock and gabion options can suit free draining retention where footprint, batter and access allow.

  • Ask each builder why the nominated system fits retained height, neighbouring loads and the water path.
  • Treat a material-only price as incomplete if drainage, footings and access are unresolved.
  • Request the proposed finish, footing detail and maintenance expectation in plain language.

Make drainage visible in the quote

Water is often the hidden reason a wall moves. A comparable quote should state the aggregate, filter fabric, subsoil pipe and lawful discharge point, then show how surface grading meets the finished alignment. For replacement work, demolition, spoil cartage, structural preparation, approvals and exclusions should be separated rather than bundled. For a new wall, ask how runoff from paving or lawn will be directed away from the retained face.

  • Near a boundary, driveway or fence, weigh drainage beside retained height and the adjoining property position.
  • Check that downpipes, agi drains or site cut-offs are not left as vague allowances.
  • If stains or sandy washouts appear after rain, photograph them before the first visit.

Set permits and boundaries early

In Victoria, a building permit is generally required for a retaining wall 1 metre or more high; lower walls can still trigger approval when tied to other building work or when protecting adjoining property. A registered building surveyor should assess the site rather than guesswork ruling the job. Planning approval remains separate, with overlays, zoning, easements and title restrictions able to change what is buildable. A boundary wall deserves early review because excavation or retention may affect neighbouring land. If an easement crosses the line, check title and required authority or council consent before fixing the wall position.

Keep the conversation evidence based and the scope comparable.

  1. Measure. Record exposed height, length and steps; add wide and close photos.
  2. Constrain. List boundary, easement, driveway, fence and access limits.
  3. Brief. Send one written pack to each builder with the same facts.
  4. Compare. Score drainage, structure, approvals, spoil and exclusions, not just total price.

Common questions

What should I send before asking for a retaining wall quote? Send suburb or postcode, approximate exposed height and length, whether the wall exists or is proposed, photos of defects and access, boundary or easement notes, and any plans. Consistent information makes each response easier to compare.

Is a permit always needed for a 1 metre wall? In Victoria a building permit is generally required at 1 metre or more, but approval questions can also arise for lower walls associated with other work or protecting adjoining property. A registered building surveyor should assess the specific site.

Why do similar walls receive different prices? Retained height, soil and slope, water management, footing or reinforcement needs, demolition and spoil, access for machinery, material choice and approval requirements can each move the figure. An itemised scope shows which differences are real.
